Episode 2: How Loop Earplugs Got Its CFO to Fund Brand Building

November 16, 2026 · 16:01 · Watch on YouTube (opens in a new tab)

About this episode

Episode 2 of Previously On, the monthly show about the business behind branded entertainment, covering Issues 21 to 25 of the Brief. This month: BERO’s and happy’s Marvel cans, Urban Decay’s Halloween series made directly with ReelShort, Christina Aguilera’s TikTok Shop launch for Playground, the job listings that show who now owns TikTok Shop sales, the ad agencies making films and series of their own, and a breakdown of how Loop Earplugs moved 10% of its budget into brand building after agreeing with its CFO on how to judge it.

Every Tuesday between episodes, The Branded Entertainment Brief covers the deals as they happen. For a closer look at the series format Urban Decay, Sol de Janeiro and 7-Eleven used, see branded microdramas.

Chapters

Transcript

Cold open

Brands are becoming entertainment companies. Here's what's working, what isn't, and why it matters. Here's what's ahead in this episode. First, three brand activations: two celebrity-founded brands selling on Marvel's release calendar, Urban Decay's Halloween series, and Christina Aguilera's TikTok Shop launch. Then Commissioning Signals, which is where the jobs brands posted this month show who now answers for creator sales. Then who's building, because ad agencies have started making films and series of their own. And then I'll take one decision apart: how Loop Earplugs found the money for brand building and got its CFO to agree to it.

The month’s three brand activations

Of the activations I tracked this month, three stood out. The first is two celebrity-founded brands selling on Marvel's release calendar. On October 8, BERO, Tom Holland's non-alcoholic beer, put its Spider-Man: Brand New Day cans back on sale, two days after the film reached digital storefronts. The first run came out with the theatrical premiere in late July. It sold out within two weeks and lifted traffic to BERO's site 121%, according to the brand. The same morning, happy, the canned coffee Robert Downey Jr. co-founded with Craig Dubitsky, launched eight collectible Avengers: Doomsday cans at Kroger, every Albertsons banner, HEB, and select Target and CVS stores. Downey plays Doctor Doom, and he's on one of the eight. The film opens December 18. A week earlier, Pop Secret put the same Doomsday license on limited-edition popcorn boxes, with an $8 Fandango reward for buying three, in many of the same stores. BERO and happy have an advantage Pop Secret can't buy with a licensing agreement alone. Their founders are in the films on the packaging, so each brand can turn an existing fan relationship into product interest. Pop Secret has to create a different incentive, a movie reward for buying three boxes. All three are turning a studio's release schedule into a retail calendar, and BERO gets another date when Spider-Man reaches disc on December 15. The second activation went a very different way. Urban Decay premiered "Ghost Hunter Hotline" on ReelShort on October 24, six short episodes about a woman whose Ghosting Spray makes the men who ghosted her disappear. L'Oréal USA developed, produced and distributed with ReelShort, without a creative agency, in about four weeks. Urban Decay says it drew 3.8 million views in five days and reached number two on ReelShort's most-viewed ranking. Maybelline's holiday series last year went through an agency. This time, L'Oréal "wanted to test directly with a platform," in the words of Han Wen, its chief digital and marketing officer in the U.S. And an agency might have data on how Urban Decay's past ads performed. ReelShort knew exactly how long its viewers stay with an episode and where the cliffhanger has to fall. ReelShort is on track for more than a billion dollars in revenue this year, by third-party estimates that Marketing Dive cites, and I think we'll see more brands produce directly with the platform that airs the show. Urban Decay wasn't the only brand with a series this month, and the others each got theirs made a different way. Sol de Janeiro went through a broadcaster. Channel 4 commissioned "The Guest List," five vertical episodes about four friends in their twenties, as its first vertical drama, and the media agency Zenith UK brokered the deal. Each friend matches one of Sol de Janeiro's new perfume mists, and one of the scents, Cheirosa 91, doesn't go on sale until December, so the series introduces it before anyone can buy it. Astral Tequila went through its parent company. Diageo's in-house entertainment team, which talks with studios year-round, won Astral a placement in Warner Bros.' Practical Magic 2. Astral then built a five-episode series around its Midnight Margarita, shot in a single day, which has passed 6 million YouTube views. And 7-Eleven made its series count at the register. "A Convenient Love," a seven-part K-drama shot in a working store, runs on 7-Eleven and Speedway's Instagram and TikTok. Each 60-second episode features a dish from its Flavors of Korea menu with a limited-time discount code, so 7-Eleven can track what each episode sells. The third activation was a livestream that worked as a storefront. On October 19, Playground, the intimate-care brand Christina Aguilera joined as co-founder in 2023, opened its TikTok Shop with an hourlong livestream on her account, which has more than 5 million followers. TikTok helped produce it, and it approved the shop even though its U.S. policy limits products like lubricants to approved sellers. The introduction came through Roc Nation, which has represented Aguilera since 2020. Her business manager there, Sarah Francis, invested in Playground when Aguilera joined, and she arranged and sat in on the meetings with TikTok. As CEO Catherine Magee told Beauty Independent, "Not only did they approve our shop, they helped us build it." Then Ulta moved Playground's new $22 cleanser up from the first quarter of 2027 to launch on its site the next day. Aguilera brought Playground more than a face for the stream. Her management company already had a line into TikTok, and a business manager who owns part of the brand had a reason to use it. The common thread this month is that each brand chose who it worked with as carefully as what it made. BERO and happy sold on films their founders star in, Urban Decay went straight to the platform, Sol de Janeiro, Astral and 7-Eleven each found their own route to a series, and Playground used a relationship its founder already had.

Commissioning Signals

Segment two is Commissioning Signals. Every week I read the job listings for creator and content roles, because the level a brand hires at, and what the job owns, shows how seriously it's building. Last episode, two brands, MAËLYS and Nood, had tied a creator job to TikTok Shop sales. This month that idea spread, and the listings pointed to three signals. The first signal: TikTok Shop is becoming a P&L that a named person owns. P&G is hiring a TikTok Shop manager to help build the first in-house TikTok Shop team for P&G Beauty. That person leads people from communications, IT, media, design, finance and product supply, and answers for sales, profit and what P&G calls "talkability." AG1's senior manager for creator commerce owns the TikTok Shop P&L alongside its Trybe creator program. Tangle Teezer wants one manager to launch its TikTok Shop and own the P&L, the affiliate commission tiers, the creator briefs, and the paid media behind the content that wins. And Pact's new director of social commerce reports to the chief digital officer. When P&G builds its own TikTok Shop team, creator content has become a sales channel with a P&L. The second signal: creators, affiliates and paid media are landing under one owner. Etsy is hiring one senior leader over both its in-house creator program and its affiliate and publisher partners, including LTK, ShopMy and YouTube Affiliate. At RUTI, a fashion brand for women 35 and older, the influencer marketing manager runs gifting, affiliate, paid and user-generated content, and tracks each creator's revenue, clicks and output every week. At Mercury, the head of social and community holds social, community, creators and merch in one budget, with measurement. The pattern is that the person picking creators now also sees what those creators sell. The third signal comes from the other side of the table: the studios and publishers are hiring creator executives. FOX hired two senior vice presidents for FOX Creator Studios, including Bryan Thoensen, who ran content partnerships at TikTok. The other, Kirsten McAuliffe, reports into entertainment ad sales, and her job is turning creator IP into advertising, licensing and franchise deals. Sony named Fede Goldenberg, YouTube's head of TV and film content partnerships, to run Screen Gems, where it will make theatrical films with digital-native creators. And The Washington Post is hiring a director to negotiate creator agreements and run creator-led series. For a brand, that means new sellers: networks that package creator franchises for advertisers. One caution: these are job descriptions and listings. They tell you what a company intends, not always what it ends up doing.

Who’s building

Last episode, the companies building creator businesses were a talent agency, a live events company and a film studio. This month it was the ad agencies, and they're moving their clients' money into the production itself. Dentsu's global head of entertainment, Cathy Boxall, went to MIPCOM in October with client briefs in hand. Dentsu is also producer, financier and lead marketer on Angry Birds Three, with two unnamed clients written into the film. Boxall says entertainment has moved out of the innovation budget and into brand strategy. As an investor, Dentsu can offer its clients integrations before anyone else. A week later, Wieden and Kennedy launched a division to develop films and series, with and without brands. Ad Age reports it starts with a Bad Bunny film. And the creator agency LV8 merged with Mirage, a network of more than 70 channels it owns across social, free streaming TV and the web. A brand hiring LV8 now buys the campaign and a place to run it from the same company. The creator commerce agency SuperOrdinary did the same thing on TikTok. In October it released "Caldwell Academy," a 44-episode scripted series, through its new studio, SuperLab, with three more originals due by the end of the year. Part of what's driving this is the price. Cyndi Steele, who leads &Beyond Creators' Studio at G&B Digital Management, puts a ten-episode creator series, with 30 short cuts, at about $300,000 to $500,000. Ten forty-minute episodes of unscripted TV cost about $5 million in physical production alone. Her brand partners usually don't own the IP, but they get first refusal on later seasons. The platforms are producing too. Spotify Studios produced "The Goop Guide With Gwyneth Paltrow," which premiered November 11. The video runs only on Netflix and Spotify, and Spotify sells the advertising around it. So Goop supplies the founder and the point of view, and the platform handles production and ad sales. And Netflix bought a series the creator Druski paid for himself, "Collect & Praise," then signed his company, 4LIFERS Entertainment, to a multi-year deal and licensed his back catalog. Netflix had turned down several of his projects before. "Now, it's a bidding war," he said. Brands are making series under their own names too. Banana Republic released "The Agency," a five-part TikTok comedy with Jack Innanen, under its Banana Republic Productions name, made in about two weeks. OUAI shot a three-part horror comedy with Victoria Pedretti in its own Los Angeles offices. Druski, Dentsu and OUAI each have paid for the first episode, and the party that pays for the first episode gets to decide what is made.

The breakdown

This month's breakdown is about money rather than a single activation, because it answers the question I hear most often, which is where does the budget for branded entertainment come from? When Rob Weston became chief commercial and marketing officer at Loop Earplugs three years ago, the Belgian brand was doing about $100 million a year and leaning heavily on Meta ads. On the D2C Diaries podcast, he put the risk this way: "The golden goose of Meta isn't necessarily going to keep laying eggs forever." The budget: Loop moved about 10% of its marketing budget from performance ads to creators and brand building. It planned that money to break even, at a return on ad spend of 1, against the 2.5 to 3 it expects from performance. Weston called it "an insurance bet." The partners: Loop signed people it had already seen using its product. It spotted Harry Styles wearing its earplugs at Glastonbury before there was any deal. The pair it later made with his brand, Pleasing, paid a minimum fee plus a revenue share that breaks even at known number of units. The measurement: before a YouTube test, Loop's CEO and CFO agreed to judge it over a 12-week window, on traffic, attributed brand search, brand lift and conversion lift. Weston says brand effects can take 18 months to show, which he calls very difficult to balance with investors, so the 12-week checkpoints cover the stretch in between. The outcome: Loop is on track for $240 million to $250 million this year. Those are Loop's own figures, and Weston still calls a single creator post "very hard to measure." Weston's real decision was when to judge the spending. A creator chosen for influence, and a collaboration built to break even on units, would probably lose on a short attribution window. So he took them out of that comparison and gave finance different readings, on dates both sides had already agreed to. For contrast, look at American Eagle. It credited its controversial July 2025 Sydney Sweeney campaign with 790,000 new U.S. customers in six weeks. But data Adweek reviewed shows that lift fading by this spring: American Eagle's comparable sales fell 2% in the first quarter and 1% in the second. The controversy bought attention quickly, and the results didn't endure. Loop decided in advance how long it would wait and what it would count, which is what lets a brand that grew on Meta ads pay for brand building without holding it to the return of the ads it competes with.

Close

So, the month. BERO and happy, Urban Decay and Playground each chose who they worked with as carefully as what they made. P&G, AG1 and Tangle Teezer are hiring people to own TikTok Shop as a P&L. Dentsu, Wieden and Kennedy and LV8 moved into production, Spotify produced a brand's new show, and Netflix signed a creator to make originals. And Loop moved 10% of its budget into brand building after agreeing with finance on how to judge it. Two things I'm watching this month. On November 16, Meta opens Series, its tool for bundling Reels into episodes on Facebook and Instagram, starting with selected creators in certain markets. Bob Evans is one of the first brands on it, with "Christmas at Maple Hollow Inn," 20 episodes and 38 minutes in all, with its mashed potatoes at the center of the plot. And Disney ran its first Super Brand Day on TikTok Shop from November 4 to 10, with Disney Store and more than 20 licensees, including Old Navy, Crocs, ColourPop, selling their Disney collections in one event. If Disney or one of those licensees shares results, we'll learn whether a licensor's TikTok Shop event can become a regular sales window.
